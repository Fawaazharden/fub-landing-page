Build log · Follow Up Boss implementation

3,272 contacts. Eight agents working live deals. One wrong text and we would have damaged their business.

This is the complete record of a Follow Up Boss AI implementation for Prime Realty Partners, a South Jersey brokerage. It covers what worked, what broke, what we shipped anyway, and what is still not solved. Ask the other vendors on your shortlist for the same document. See what comes back.

The situation

They did not have a lead problem. They had eight people and no way to be first.

Prime Realty Partners runs Follow Up Boss as its system of record. Leads arrive from paid social and from their own IDX website. Eight licensed agents are organised into three territory groups, with two to three eligible agents covering each area.

Every path needed a decision

An answered call, a missed call, a listings request and a seller conversation are four different situations. Each one needed a defined next action that did not depend on which agent happened to pick it up.

Handoff had to respect territory

Eight agents, three groups, and contested towns that legitimately belong to more than one. A generic round robin would have handed a Cherry Hill lead to a Burlington agent and burned the relationship.

An incumbent was already in place

An AI tool was already assigned to their warm leads. They were evaluating a replacement rather than buying their first one, which meant the bar was not “does AI work”. It was “is this better than what we have”.

The finding that reframed the project

We audited 312 recent leads in their account before writing any code. Five of them had any property activity recorded. Not five percent. Five leads. That is not a team failing at their jobs, it is what happens when following up depends on a human being free at the exact moment a stranger decides to reply. We reported the gap rather than turning it into an intent score we could not justify.

The constraint

The hardest requirement was not a feature. It was that nothing could go wrong once.

Their agents were working real deals in that CRM throughout the build. There is no staging copy of a live brokerage. A stray text does not get rolled back, it reaches a person who is selling their house, and it lands in front of somebody who is selling their house, and the client hears about it from them before they hear about it from us.

So the guard went in before anything that could send. It refuses to deliver to anybody except one designated test contact, it fails closed when configuration is missing, and it throws loudly instead of skipping quietly.

  • Every outbound path routes through the same check: texts, calls, CRM writes, and the agent dial legs.
  • The default in code is blocked. A wiped settings file or a fresh deploy is safe.
  • 28 assertions run before every deploy, driving every outbound path at a number that is not the test contact and proving each one refuses.
  • Agent phone legs stayed pointed at a controlled number, so a live transfer test never rang a real agent’s mobile.

Why this matters to you

If you run a real book of business, ask the vendor what stops the AI saying anything to the wrong person. Then ask what happens on a fresh deploy with an empty configuration file. If the answer is that it starts sending, that is your answer.

What was built

One system, not five integrations stitched together after the fact.

Both channels read from the same conversation history, so a lead who said something on the phone is never asked it again by text. That single decision removed most of what makes AI follow up feel robotic.

01

Call qualification

Separate buyer and seller paths with the client’s approved questions, defined outcomes and escalation rules. Voicemail detected as voicemail rather than counted as a conversation.

02

Missed call recovery

When a lead declined or missed the call, the system continued by text instead of dialling again. In this build, the exchange that produced the qualification started here and not on the first ring.

03

Property search links

The town a lead named resolved to real ZIP codes using the client’s own mapping, including contested towns and the ways leads mishear or abbreviate place names.

04

Live transfer

Keep the lead on the line, ring every eligible agent in the territory group at once, and connect whoever answers first. The claim is a keypress.

05

CRM write back

Texts, calls, recordings, transcripts, qualification answers, funnel status and assignment, written onto the Follow Up Boss person record as they happen rather than dumped at the end.

06

Operator control

A tag on the contact switches the AI on and off for that lead, managed by staff inside the CRM. A separate operator view holds the global stop and the reporting.

One detail worth stealing regardless of who builds yours

A keypress claims the lead, never an answered call. An answering machine picks up, which means answering “wins” the lead for a voicemail box. We also do not rely on automated machine detection on the agent leg, because a late verdict can tear down a call two humans are already having.

The acceptance record

Eleven scenarios. Seven passed, two we fixed mid test, two are still open.

These are functional acceptance outcomes taken from the implementation record. There is no conversion data here, and the last section of this page explains exactly why.

New lead to first contactAcceptance test

The Follow Up Boss webhook fired and the introduction text was sent 2.4 seconds later. Speed to lead was the number this client measured us on, so it was the first thing instrumented.

Called, went to voicemailAcceptance test

The call reached a voicemail box. It was correctly classified as voicemail rather than a connected conversation, no qualification data was taken from it, and the sequence continued by text.

Inbound reply to qualificationAcceptance test

One inbound text produced four qualification facts: area, budget, must haves and timeline. All four wrote into the custom fields the client already had, rather than into new fields we invented.

Search link generationAcceptance test

The town the lead named resolved to a three ZIP band with a $340,000 to $440,000 price range, assembled from what the lead actually said rather than from a default.

CRM mirroringAcceptance test

Every step of that sequence appeared on the person record. Zero mirror failures across the run.

Live transfer, bridge includedAcceptance test

The lead asked for an agent during an AI call. The territory group rang, an agent pressed one to claim it, and the call bridged. An 84 second conversation landed on the record with its transcript, a playable recording, the call outcome, the assignment and the funnel status. Post call analysis then reported the lead still wanted an agent, and the system correctly declined to ring anybody a second time because the claim had already happened.

Activation model, on a real leadAcceptance test

A genuine inbound lead from their live source arrived while the build was still in progress. The system ingested it, logged it, and sent nothing and wrote nothing to that person’s record, because the activation tag was absent. Nobody was watching when it happened. That is the strongest evidence on this page.

Contact window enforcementFixed during testing

Testing on his own contact, the broker got a text and a call late in the evening. Nothing in the first build had looked at the clock. Nothing outbound now goes to a lead outside 8am to 9pm in their local time, and a step that comes due outside that window is deferred to the next opening instead of cancelled.

Reply pacingFixed during testing

Replies now wait 25 to 35 seconds, measured from the lead’s message rather than added after drafting, so a slow model does not produce a later reply than a fast one.

Appointment bookingCaveat

Booking checks agent availability against the CRM calendar. When this was built the account had no future appointments on any agent, so the conflict check is correct but not yet protective. If a team keeps its real diary in Google and never syncs it, this will believe agents are free when they are not. The agent veto by text is the backstop and getting calendar sync switched on is the real fix.

Single thread introductionNot delivered

The client asked for the agent and the lead to be introduced in one group thread. The carrier rejects the group message outright. What happens instead is a matched pair of texts in two separate threads. It reads fine and nothing is broken, but it is not what was asked for, and pretending otherwise would be the kind of thing this page exists to avoid.

What went wrong

Five defects, found on a test contact, fixed before a real lead was in scope.

The broker created a test contact for himself and ran the system for about an hour. Five defects surfaced, before any real lead was in scope. Two of them changed the design and not just the code.

1. The AI invented an answer from its own voicemail

On an unanswered call the transcript is the machine greeting plus our own voicemail message and nothing else. The post call analysis read our words back as the lead’s answer and reported an area preference the lead had never given. It merged as a fact, wrote onto the contact record as their stated preference, counted toward qualification progress, and got built into the search link.

The second order damage is the one that matters. A question that reads as answered is never asked again, so the lead is never asked properly and nobody ever finds out. On any lead nobody inspected by hand, this would have been invisible.

Fixed by merging facts only from calls that actually connected, and by rewriting the analysis instructions so the AI’s own words are never treated as evidence.
2. A 22 second brush off was logged as an interested lead

The entire content of the call was “is now an ok time?” and “not really”. It was written to the CRM with the outcome Interested, because the outcome had been decided on call duration alone. That is the field the client’s agents filter on to decide who is worth ringing, so the cost lands on a human wasting an afternoon on a lead who had said no.

Fixed so Interested requires actual evidence of interest: a question answered, or a request for an agent, a showing or a callback.
3. Bad timing was recorded as refusing an agent

Saying “not really” to “is now an ok time” put a tag on the contact and a note on the record saying he had turned down a live transfer. He had never been offered one. It also moved him onto the never offer an agent again path. Bad timing is the most common answer an unscheduled call gets, so this was mislabelling the majority of first calls.

Fixed with a separate bad timing signal, checked before the declined path, that writes no tag, claims no refusal and sends no link.
4. Three identical introduction texts in 1.7 seconds

One lead was introduced to the AI three times in under two seconds, each message announcing itself for the first time. Two schedulers were running at once by design, and the step was being marked done after the send rather than before it, so it sat pending for the few seconds the draft took and the second scheduler picked it up and ran it again.

The copy checker had actually flagged both duplicates and sent anyway, which is correct behaviour under its own rule that a weak text beats no text. It also meant a concurrency bug looked like a wording problem for longer than it should have.

Fixed by claiming the step synchronously before anything can await, with a stale claim released after a short timeout so a crashed process cannot strand a lead’s sequence forever.
5. A property link built from nothing

A lead who had answered none of the qualification questions received a search link one message into the relationship. With no area to work from it resolved to the widest possible default: eight ZIP codes across three counties, no price, no bed count, no property type. The client’s own description was “a link to properties with not much context”.

Underneath it was a prompt problem worth knowing about. One part of the prompt removed the question list for that message type and another part quietly put it back, so a message specified to ask nothing went out asking a question and dropped two of the three things it was supposed to say.

Fixed so a link only goes out when the lead actually named an area, and so the message asks the single question that would let us keep the promise instead.

Four rounds of client feedback

Every round was about judgement, not bugs. Those are the expensive ones.

Once the mechanics worked, none of the remaining feedback was about whether a feature functioned. It was about how hard the AI pushed, when a human should be pulled in, and whether it sounded like a person. That is the part a demo cannot show you.

Round 1

“Not much context”

Messages that did not say who was writing or why. The fix was a behavioural standard both channels inherit: name yourself, say why this is arriving now, ask one specific thing.

The part worth knowing: writing the rule into the prompt did not hold it. Nine real runs, and four of them broke a rule the prompt explicitly forbade. So the rule became code that checks the finished draft and forces one redraft, instead of an instruction we hoped the model would follow.

Round 2

“It can feel bot like”

His words: “Speed in which the AI responds via text. Its so fast it can feel bot like.” Replies now wait 25 to 35 seconds, measured from the lead’s message.

What that pause broke: the global stop can be switched on during the wait, and the lead can keep typing. So the stop gets re read after the pause, and if a newer message has landed the older reply stands down, because the newer one has read more of the conversation.

Round 3

“Handoff happens at that moment, not before”

He gave us the governing rule himself: the AI owns the lead until the lead is ready to speak to an agent or see a property. Two things changed. The AI now asks once whether they want an agent rather than deciding for them, and a showing request gets a day and a time booked before any agent is contacted.

Before this, an agent’s phone rang for “can I see that one” with no day, no time and nothing agreed, so the agent had to call the lead back to ask what the AI could have asked itself.

Round 4

“Separating the link from the last text”

He was right about the mechanics. A long search URL on top of prose pushes the message across several SMS segments, and a handset that reassembles them badly can break the URL across a boundary and leave it unclickable.

Order matters, so this is a sequence and not two sends fired together. Prose first, then the link. Reversed, the link arrives before anything has said what it is, which is round one all over again.

What is still open

Three things still not right, including one the carrier will not let us fix.

Every implementation has one. Most case studies leave it out. That is why they are worth nothing to you.

No business outcome data

No appointments, closings, revenue or conversion lift are claimed for this client, because none of it is verified. A cohort rollout was proposed and the numbers to publish do not exist yet. When they do, they go here.

Calendar sync is not on

The availability check cannot protect anybody until real commitments actually appear in the CRM calendar. Until then the agent veto by text is doing that job on its own.

The group thread is two threads

The carrier rejects the group message. The introduction works as a matched pair of texts, which is not what was specified, and the client has been told rather than left to notice.

Why this page has no conversion chart

Because we do not have one for this client, and the ones you have been shown elsewhere are usually a blended average across accounts with different lead sources, different scripts and different follow up discipline. A number like that tells you nothing about what your database would do. The acceptance record above is worth more, because you can check whether the same rigour would be applied to yours.
